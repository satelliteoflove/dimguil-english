Who Gaura is to a Japanese player

Research for the Gaura voice scene, 2026-10-20. What his Japanese, his portrait and the game's own touchpoints would have told a player in 2000, and what that means for his English.

There's no fan writing about Gaura online that I could find: no character discussions, no trope labels. So this is put together from the game itself and from well-documented Japanese conventions, not from what fans said.

He isn't one named stock character. He's a combination a Japanese reader would recognise at once: a shy, kind, non-human big guy who speaks the language haltingly, with a childlike Wizardry dream attached. The piece I think we were missing is that his broken speech reads at least as much "not his native language" as "simple-minded."

1. The speech: 片言, the non-native kind

Japanese fiction has an established way to write 片言 (katakoto, "broken speech"): writing words in katakana, dropping particles and auxiliaries, and chopping speech into short pieces. It's used for foreigners (the stock 「ワタシ、〜ネ」), robots (「オレタチハ、ろぼっとダ」), and monsters or non-humans. The pixiv encyclopedia calls it a Showa-era stereotype. Writers on character voice note the same technique can also signal simple-mindedness.

Gaura's markers, line by line:

The katakoto in the Japanese is light. It sits in the katakana, the commas and the missing だ, not in mangled grammar. To a Japanese reader that says "a big non-human who talks a bit like a foreigner," not "an idiot." That fits him being a Mook. In Wizardry VI and VII, Mooks are an alien race of tall, hairy, Sasquatch-like people, often compared to Star Wars Wookiees. Japanese players knew them from the Japanese releases of those games.

2. The type: the shy kind giant

The general type is 気は優しくて力持ち, "kind of heart and strong of body," named after a line in the Momotaro song (1900). Standard examples include Yamada Taro in Dokaben and Fudo of the Mountain in Fist of the North Star. Gaura bends it in two ways.

  • He's timid, not protective. Classic examples are gentle but brave. Gaura finds everyone おっかない ("scary"), and he ran away from Balbo (5:66). That makes him the "looks frightening, is actually the frightened one" variant.
  • He's a Mage, not a strongman. His battle record makes him sturdy for a Mage (32-38 HP at level 6), but his job is magic. The robe, hood and gnarled staff in his portrait say hermit-wizard, not brawler.

Western readers will think of Chewbacca or Bigfoot for the look, and perhaps the Cowardly Lion from The Wizard of Oz for a big furry figure who is scared of everything and longs for courage. Those are resonances, not sources: nothing ties Gaura to them.

3. The touchpoint a Wizardry fan would catch: the Samurai dream

This is probably the most "obvious to the intended audience" signal, and it's a Wizardry one, not an anime one.

  • Samurai is the elite class. In Dimguil it needs ST 12, IQ 11, VT 9, AG 14 and LK 9, and a Good or Neutral alignment.
  • Mooks are slow. Their base agility is 7, tied with Dwarves for second lowest (only Gnomes are lower), and the class needs 14. Gaura himself says he's slow (動くの遅い) and envies how fast everyone moves.
  • The Muramasa is the legendary prize. In Japanese Wizardry culture the Muramasa Blade is the ultimate sword, the item players famously grind for and rarely get. Dimguil has it too.

So 「オ,オレ,侍になったら ム,ムラマサ手に入れるんだ。」, "when I'm a Samurai I'm gonna get the Muramasa," is a kid's dream: the slow, timid Mage who wants to be the fastest, bravest class with the rarest sword. A Wizardry player would find that touching and a little funny, not stupid. It's the same dream they had.

4. A smaller touch: the name

Many of Dimguil's NPCs are named after plants: Gaillardia (Guy), Linaria, Lychee, Artemisia, Fritillaria, Bergamot, Cresson (French for watercress), and Fontana's real name Kikunoshin (菊ノ進, with 菊, chrysanthemum). Gaura is a garden flower too, sold in Japan as 白蝶草, "white butterfly flower." It has small white blossoms that sway like a cloud of butterflies, and its flower-language meaning is 清楚, "pure, neat, innocent." A delicate white flower for a big white-furred, gentle man reads like a deliberate, affectionate joke.

The plant-name pattern is my reading of the cast list, and I haven't checked every NPC. The flower language comes from Japanese gardening sites.

What this means for his English

  • Keep him shy, slow and kind, as planned. The stammer and the sounds are his.
  • A touch of non-native English is right; caveman English isn't. An occasional missing article or a very plain construction fits a Mook speaking a language that isn't his. "Me, Gaura" and "Kid, but he leader" cross into Tarzan territory, which the Japanese doesn't. In English, "me" for "I" reads as a lack of intelligence far more than katakana オレ does in Japanese.
  • Let the Samurai dream land as a dream. "I-I'm g-gonna be a Samurai. Someday." and "W-when I'm a Samurai, I'm g-gonna get a Muramasa." carry it, and every Wizardry player in English knows the Muramasa too.
  • Don't make him dim. He's a Mage: he studies (2:192 notices the scholars' books), he hopes to talk to your dead friend next time (2:43), and he's generous about others (2:229). Simple words, a warm mind.
